### 1. What the Problem is Asking

We are asked to rank five different carbonyl compounds (aldehydes and ketones with various substituents) in order of their reactivity toward **nucleophilic addition**. 

Nucleophilic addition to a carbonyl group ($\text{C=O}$) depends on two main factors:
1. **Electronic effects:** The electrophilicity (positive charge character) of the carbonyl carbon. Electron-withdrawing groups increase this positive charge and speed up the reaction, while electron-donating groups decrease it and slow down the reaction.
2. **Steric effects:** The size of the groups attached to the carbonyl carbon. Bulky groups crowd the reaction center and hinder the approach of a nucleophile, slowing down the reaction.

We need to analyze how substituents and the fundamental difference between aldehydes and ketones affect these factors to determine the correct reactivity sequence.

### 2. Step-by-Step Analysis

To determine the correct order, let's analyze each compound based on electronic and steric factors:

#### Step 1: Understand the general reactivity trends
* **Aldehydes vs. Ketones:** Aldehydes ($\text{RCHO}$) are generally **more reactive** than ketones ($\text{RCOR}'$) toward nucleophilic addition for two reasons:
  * *Electronic:* Ketones have two electron-donating alkyl groups attached to the carbonyl carbon, which stabilize (reduce the positive charge of) the electrophilic center more than the single alkyl/aryl group in an aldehyde (plus a hydrogen atom).
  * *Steric:* Ketones have two bulky groups attached to the carbonyl carbon, whereas aldehydes have one small hydrogen atom, making the carbonyl carbon in aldehydes much more accessible to an incoming nucleophile.
* **Benzaldehydes vs. Aliphatic Ketones / Diaryl Ketones:** While aromatic aldehydes are less reactive than simple aliphatic aldehydes due to resonance stabilization with the benzene ring, how do they compare to ketones? Let's look closely at the specific compounds provided.

#### Step 2: Analyze the specific compounds
Let's list the five compounds and evaluate their electronic and steric environments:

* **(A) $p$-Nitrobenzaldehyde ($\text{O}_2\text{N-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-CHO}$):**
  * The nitro group ($\text{-NO}_2$) is a very strong **electron-withdrawing group** (both $-M$ and $-I$ effects). 
  * This significantly depletes electron density from the carbonyl carbon, making it *highly* electrophilic.
  * **Status:** This will be the **most reactive** compound by far ($\text{A} > \text{others}$).

* **(B) Benzaldehyde ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-CHO}$):**
  * Unsubstituted aromatic aldehyde. The benzene ring has a weak +M resonance effect that slightly stabilizes the carbonyl carbon compared to aliphatic aldehydes, but it still has only one aryl group and an H atom attached to the carbonyl.

* **(C) $p$-Methoxybenzaldehyde ($\text{CH}_3\text{O-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-CHO}$):**
  * The methoxy group ($\text{-OCH}_3$) at the para position is a strong **electron-donating group via resonance** ($+M$ effect). 
  * This pushes electron density toward the carbonyl carbon, significantly reducing its electrophilicity.

* **(D) Butan-2-one ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{COCH}_3$):**
  * An aliphatic ketone. It has two alkyl groups ($\text{-CH}_3$ and $\text{-CH}_2\text{CH}_3$) attached to the carbonyl carbon.
  * Aliphatic ketones are generally less reactive than aldehydes due to steric hindrance and hyperconjugative/inductive electron donation from the alkyl groups. However, *is the electronic deactivation in $p$-methoxybenzaldehyde strong enough to push it below an aliphatic ketone?*
  * Let's check experimental data: Nucleophilic addition rates (such as cyanohydrin formation or bisulfite addition) show that even with an electron-donating methoxy group, $p$-methoxybenzaldehyde remains more reactive than simple aliphatic ketones like butan-2-one. This is because the steric hindrance in ketones (two alkyl groups) combined with the extreme accessibility of the aldehyde hydrogen ($\text{-H}$) keeps aldehydes structurally primed for faster nucleophile attack.

* **(E) Benzophenone ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COC}_6\text{H}_5$):**
  * A diaryl ketone. It has two bulky phenyl rings attached to the carbonyl carbon, providing massive steric hindrance and extensive resonance stabilization of the carbonyl carbon.
  * **Status:** This is the **least reactive** compound ($\text{E}$ is last).

#### Step 3: Compare $p$-Methoxybenzaldehyde (C) and Butan-2-one (D)
The student's core dilemma is whether the strong $+M$ effect of the $\text{-OCH}_3$ group in (C) makes it less reactive than the aliphatic ketone (D). 

While the $\text{-OCH}_3$ group deactivates the benzaldehyde ring electronically, benzaldehydes maintain a critical structural advantage: **steric hindrance is much lower in aldehydes than in ketones.** Butan-2-one has two bulky alkyl chains flanking the $\text{C=O}$ carbon, whereas $p$-methoxybenzaldehyde has one flat aromatic ring and one tiny hydrogen atom. The transition state for nucleophilic addition involves moving from trigonal planar ($sp^2$) to tetrahedral ($sp^3$), which is much more sterically demanding for ketones than for aldehydes. 

Therefore, the low steric hindrance of the aldehyde functional group outweighs the electronic deactivation of the $p$-methoxy group, keeping (C) more reactive than the ketone (D).

Thus, the original book order is actually correct.

### 3. Final Answer

The correct relative order of rates of nucleophilic addition is:

$$\mathbf{A > B > C > D > E}$$

*(where **A** = $p$-Nitrobenzaldehyde, **B** = Benzaldehyde, **C** = $p$-Methoxybenzaldehyde, **D** = Butan-2-one, and **E** = Benzophenone)*

### 4. Common Mistakes for This Problem Type

1. **Ignoring Steric Effects:** Assuming reactivity can be predicted *solely* by looking at $+M/-M$ or $+I/-I$ electronic effects. Steric accessibility of the carbonyl carbon plays a massive role, which is why aldehydes almost always outpace ketones of similar electronic profiles.
2. **Overestimating Substituent Power:** Assuming that a strong electron-donating group (like $\text{-OCH}_3$) can completely override the fundamental aldehyde vs. ketone reactivity gap. While electron donors slow down aldehydes, the difference in sterics and hybridization between aldehydes and ketones usually keeps the aldehyde ahead of simple aliphatic ketones.
3. **Confusing Aromatic and Aliphatic Ketones:** Forgetting that diaryl ketones (like benzophenone, $\text{E}$) are exceptionally unreactive due to both severe steric hindrance from two large phenyl rings and extensive resonance delocalization across both rings.
