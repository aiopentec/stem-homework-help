## 1.  What the question is asking (in plain language)

The student wants to know  

* **Is the “aglycone” part of a glycoside allowed to be a single sugar molecule (a monosaccharide)?**  
* **If the answer is “yes”, could a disaccharide such as maltose be called a glycoside?**  

In other words: does the definition *glycone + aglycone* require the aglycone to be **non‑carbohydrate**, or can it be a sugar itself?

---

## 2.  Step‑by‑step reasoning  

### Step 1 – Recall the IUPAC definition of a glycoside  

> **Glycoside** – a compound in which a **glycone** (a carbohydrate fragment derived from a monosaccharide) is linked through a **glycosidic bond** to a **non‑carbohydrate moiety**, called the **aglycone** (or genin).  
> – *IUPAC Recommendations, 2013, Section 2.5.1.*

Key points:

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Glycone** | The carbohydrate part; it may be a single monosaccharide or an oligosaccharide (disaccharide, trisaccharide …). |
| **Aglycone** | Anything *that is not a carbohydrate* (e.g., phenyl, alkyl, terpenoid, steroid, etc.). |
| **Glycosidic bond** | The covalent link (usually O‑, N‑, or S‑) that connects the anomeric carbon of the glycone to the aglycone. |

Thus, by the *official* definition the aglycone **cannot be a sugar**.

---

### Step 2 – What happens when *two* sugars are linked?

When two monosaccharides are joined through their anomeric carbon(s) we obtain a **disaccharide** (or, more generally, an oligosaccharide).  
The bond between the two sugars is *still* called a **glycosidic bond**, but the whole molecule is classified as a **saccharide**, not a glycoside, because **both fragments are carbohydrates**.

Therefore:

* The first sugar is not called a “glycone” in the sense used for glycosides; it is simply the *reducing* part of the disaccharide.  
* The second sugar is not an “aglycone”; it is another **glycone** fragment.  

The term *aglycone* is reserved for the *non‑sugar* partner.

---

### Step 3 – Can the aglycone be a monosaccharide in any accepted usage?

* **Strict IUPAC usage:** No. The aglycone must be non‑carbohydrate.  
* **Looser, colloquial usage:** Some older textbooks loosely refer to the *non‑reducing* sugar unit of a disaccharide as the “aglycone”, but this is **not recommended** and can cause confusion. Modern chemical literature avoids that usage.

Consequences:

* **Maltose** (α‑D‑glucopyranosyl‑(1→4)‑β‑D‑glucopyranose) is a **disaccharide**, not a glycoside.  
* If you replace the second glucose by a phenyl group you obtain **phenyl‑β‑D‑glucoside**, which *is* a genuine glycoside because the phenyl moiety is non‑carbohydrate.

---

### Step 4 – Summarise the answer to the two questions  

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Can the aglycone be a monosaccharide? | **No**, by the accepted definition the aglycone must be a non‑carbohydrate fragment. |
| Can maltose be called a glycoside? | **No**, maltose is a disaccharide (a saccharide), not a glycoside. |

---

## 3.  Final answer  

- **The aglycone part of a glycoside is defined as a non‑carbohydrate moiety; it cannot be a monosaccharide.**  
- **Maltose, which consists of two glucose units linked by a glycosidic bond, is a disaccharide, not a glycoside.**  

If a sugar is attached to something that is *not* a sugar (e.g., a phenyl, a steroid, a flavonoid), the resulting molecule *is* a glycoside, and the non‑sugar part is the aglycone (or genin).

---

## 4.  Common mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to correct it |
|---------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Calling the second sugar in a disaccharide the “aglycone”.** | By definition the aglycone must be non‑carbohydrate. | Refer to both units simply as *sugar residues*; the whole molecule is a *disaccharide*. |
| **Using “glycoside” to mean any molecule that contains a glycosidic bond.** | A glycosidic bond can also join two sugars (disaccharides, oligosaccharides). The term “glycoside” is reserved for a sugar‑plus‑non‑sugar combination. | Reserve “glycoside” for structures where the non‑sugar moiety is attached to the anomeric carbon of a sugar. |
| **Assuming that a polysaccharide fragment can serve as the aglycone.** | The aglycone must be *non‑carbohydrate* regardless of size. | If the non‑sugar part is a large natural product (e.g., a steroid), it is still the aglycone; the carbohydrate part can be mono‑, di‑, or oligosaccharide. |
| **Confusing “glycone” with “single monosaccharide”.** | The glycone may be a single sugar *or* an oligosaccharide (disaccharide, trisaccharide, …). | When the carbohydrate part consists of several sugar units, we speak of an *oligoglycone* but the molecule is still a glycoside if the other part is non‑carbohydrate. |

Keeping the definitions precise prevents mixing up **saccharides** (pure carbohydrates) with **glycosides** (carbohydrate + non‑carbohydrate).
