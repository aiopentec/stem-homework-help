# Expert Chemistry Tutorial: Lewis Acids, Carbocations, and Acidity

---

## 1. Restating the Problem in Plain Language

This problem asks two main things about Lewis acids (electron-pair acceptors):
1. **The Mechanism:** Exactly how does a Lewis acid (like $\text{AlCl}_3$) pull apart a covalent bond (like a $\text{C-Cl}$ bond in an alkyl halide) to create a carbocation? What is the physical "push" or "pull" that makes this happen?
2. **Relative Strengths:** How do we rank different Lewis acids (such as $\text{AlCl}_3$, $\text{FeCl}_3$, $\text{BF}_3$, and $\text{SbF}_5$) from weakest to strongest, and what chemical properties control these differences?

---

## 2. Step-by-Step Solution

### Part 1: Mechanism of Carbocation Formation

To understand how a Lewis acid promotes carbocation formation (for example, in a Friedel-Crafts reaction), we need to look at the step-by-step interaction between an alkyl halide (like tert-butyl chloride) and a Lewis acid (like $\text{AlCl}_3$).

#### Step 1: Coordination (Lewis Acid-Base Complexation)
* **The Actors:** $\text{AlCl}_3$ has a central aluminum atom surrounded by six valence electrons (an incomplete octet), meaning it possesses a low-energy, vacant $p$ orbital. Tert-butyl chloride ($\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{CCl}$) has chlorine atoms with lone pairs of electrons.
* **The Interaction:** The chlorine atom uses one of its lone pairs to form a dative (coordinate covalent) bond by donating electron density into the vacant $p$ orbital of the aluminum atom in $\text{AlCl}_3$.
* **The Driving Force:** This step is thermodynamically favorable because a new, stable coordinate covalent bond is formed, and aluminum achieves a stable octet (though it gains a formal negative charge, while chlorine gains a formal positive charge).

#### Step 2: Polarization of the C–Cl Bond
* Once the chlorine atom coordinates to $\text{AlCl}_3$, electron density is pulled away from the chlorine atom toward the strongly electron-withdrawing $\text{AlCl}_3$ group (which acts as a massive electron sink).
* Because chlorine is now electron-deficient (bearing a partial or full positive charge), it pulls even harder on the bonding electrons it shares with the carbon atom of the alkyl group via inductive effect.
* As a result, the $\text{C–Cl}$ covalent bond becomes drastically polarized and elongated. The bonding electrons are pulled away from the carbon and localized almost entirely onto the chlorine-aluminum complex.

#### Step 3: Heterolytic Bond Cleavage and Carbocation Generation
* The $\text{C–Cl}$ bond breaks heterolytically—meaning both electrons from the sigma bond stay with the leaving group ($\text{AlCl}_4^-$).
* **The Driving Force:** Why does the bond snap? 
  1. The formation of the very stable, tetrahedral tetrachloroaluminate anion ($\text{AlCl}_4^-$) releases a significant amount of lattice/solvation energy.
  2. The carbon atom left behind adopts a planar, $sp^2$-hybridized geometry with a stable vacant $p$ orbital, often stabilized heavily by hyperconjugation and inductive electron donation from neighboring alkyl groups (e.g., tertiary carbocations).
* **Result:** A free carbocation and $\text{AlCl}_4^-$ are formed.

### Part 2: Comparing Relative Strengths of Lewis Acids

To compare Lewis acids like $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{AlCl}_3$, $\text{FeCl}_3$, and $\text{SbF}_5$, we must look at the factors that make a species eager to accept an electron pair.

#### Key Factors Determining Lewis Acidity:
1. **Energy and Availability of the Vacant Orbital:** A lower-energy LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital) accepts electrons more readily.
2. **Positive Charge / Inductive Effects:** Highly electronegative atoms attached to the central atom pull electron density away, making the central atom more electropositive and hungry for electrons.
3. **Steric Hindrance:** Bulky substituents can hinder the approach of a Lewis base, lowering the effective Lewis acidity.
4. **$\pi$-Backbonding (Internal Donation):** If atoms attached to the central atom have lone pairs that can be donated into the vacant orbital (e.g., the fluorine atoms in $\text{BF}_3$), it neutralizes some of the electron deficiency, *decreasing* Lewis acidity.

#### Analyzing Specific Lewis Acids:

* **$\text{BF}_3$ vs. $\text{AlCl}_3$:** 
  Boron is in row 2, and Aluminum is in row 3. While fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine, $\text{BF}_3$ suffers from strong **$\pi$-backbonding**: the filled $2p$ orbitals of fluorine overlap effectively with the vacant $2p$ orbital of boron, partially satisfying its electron deficiency. Chlorine (row 3) does not overlap its $3p$ orbitals nearly as well with boron's or aluminum's empty orbitals. Consequently, $\text{AlCl}_3$ is a significantly stronger Lewis acid in organic reactions than $\text{BF}_3$.
  
* **$\text{FeCl}_3$:** 
  Similar to $\text{AlCl}_3$, iron(III) chloride features a metal center with a vacant orbital and strongly electronegative chlorines. It acts as a powerful Lewis acid, comparable to $\text{AlCl}_3$.
  
* **$\text{SbF}_5$ (Antimony Pentafluoride):** 
  Antimony is a heavier, larger element in Group 15, bonded to five extremely electronegative fluorine atoms. $\text{SbF}_5$ is famously one of the strongest neutral Lewis acids known (often called a "Super-Lewis acid"). The combination of high electronegativity from five fluorines and the size/polarizability of antimony creates an intensely electropositive central atom with a very low-energy vacant orbital and virtually no effective backbonding that can stabilize it against strong bases.

#### Quantitative and Qualitative Comparison Metrics:
* **Qualitative:** Looking at periodic trends (down a group, size increases; across a period, electronegativity increases) and resonance/backbonding capabilities.
* **Quantitative:** 
  * **Fluoride Ion Affinity (FIA):** The enthalpy change ($\Delta H$) for the gas-phase reaction: $\text{Lewis Acid} + \text{F}^- \rightarrow [\text{Lewis Acid-F}]^-$. A more exothermic FIA indicates a stronger Lewis acid.
  * **The Gutmann-Beckett Method / Childress Method:** Using Lewis bases (like triethylphosphine oxide, $\text{Et}_3\text{PO}$) and measuring shifts in $^{31}\text{P}$ NMR spectroscopy to quantify how much electron density is drained by the Lewis acid.

## 3. Final Answer

1. **Mechanism of Carbocation Formation:** Lewis acids promote carbocation formation by coordinating with a leaving group (like chlorine in an alkyl chloride) via their vacant orbitals. This coordination polarizes and weakens the adjacent $\text{C–X}$ bond. Heterolytic cleavage then occurs, driven by the formation of a stable counter-anion (e.g., $\text{AlCl}_4^-$) and the generation of a stabilized carbocation.
2. **Comparing Lewis Acid Strengths:** Lewis acidity is determined by the electrophilicity of the central atom (driven by positive charge, electronegative substituents, and low-energy vacant orbitals) combined with the absence of stabilizing internal $\pi$-backbonding. 
3. **General Trend of Strengths:** 
   $$\text{BF}_3 < \text{AlCl}_3 \approx \text{FeCl}_3 \ll \text{SbF}_5$$
   (With $\text{SbF}_5$ being an extreme super-Lewis acid, and $\text{BF}_3$ being heavily moderated by effective $p-p$ $\pi$-backbonding).

## 4. Common Mistakes

* **Mistake 1: Assuming Fluorine compounds are always the strongest Lewis acids.** 
  Students often see highly electronegative fluorines in $\text{BF}_3$ and assume it is stronger than $\text{AlCl}_3$. They forget about **$\pi$-backbonding**, where fluorine's lone pairs fill the empty boron orbital, making $\text{BF}_3$ a weaker Lewis acid than $\text{AlCl}_3$.
* **Mistake 2: Thinking the Lewis acid "grabs" the carbon directly.** 
  Students sometimes draw the Lewis acid attacking the carbon atom of an alkyl halide. In reality, the Lewis acid always coordinates first to the *heteroatom* bearing the lone pair (halogen, oxygen, nitrogen), which then triggers the departure of that group.
* **Mistake 3: Confusing Brønsted-Lowry and Lewis acidity.** 
  Remember that Lewis acidity is strictly about **electron pair acceptance** (vacant orbitals), not proton ($\text{H}^+$) donation. While many strong Lewis acids react with traces of water to generate Brønsted superacids (e.g., $\text{HBF}_4$ or fluoroantimonic acid), the Lewis acidity itself is measured by affinity for electron pairs (like $\text{F}^-$).
