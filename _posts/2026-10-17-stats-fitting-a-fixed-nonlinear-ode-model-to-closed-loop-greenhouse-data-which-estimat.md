## 1.  What is being asked?  

A greenhouse climate model is already **fixed**:  
* 28 ordinary differential equations (ODEs) describing temperature, humidity, CO₂, etc.  
* ≈200 physical parameters – the student is only allowed to scale a handful of them (multiplicative factors).  

The data come from a **closed‑loop** experiment: a controller (the climate computer) continuously reads the same variables that we want to fit and decides how much heating, ventilation, shading, etc., to apply. Consequently the **input signals are correlated with the disturbances** (measurement noise, unmodelled dynamics, weather fluctuations).  

The student tried a deterministic “output‑error” (OE) fit (simulation error minimisation) and obtained a biased, poorly predictive model.  

**Question:**  
*Given a known nonlinear ODE structure and closed‑loop data, which estimation strategy should be used instead of plain OE least‑squares?*  

Possible candidates mentioned are  

* prediction‑error (PE) with a stochastic noise model,  
* multiple‑shooting,  
* Bayesian parameter estimation,  
* an estimator that explicitly uses the known controller,  

and the student would also like a concrete illustration on a physical model.

## 2.  Step‑by‑step answer  

Below we develop a **complete, practical recipe** for this class of problems.  
We start from the statistical description of the data‑generating system, identify why OE is biased, and then construct an estimator that eliminates—or at least greatly reduces—that bias.  
Every step is written out explicitly so you can reproduce it on the greenhouse model (or any other nonlinear ODE system).

### 2.1  Write down the closed‑loop system in statistical form  

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| \(x(t)\in\mathbb R^{n}\) | State vector (solution of the 28 ODEs). |
| \(\theta\in\mathbb R^{p}\) | Vector of *unknown* parameters (the 8 scaling factors). |
| \(u(t)\in\mathbb R^{m}\) | Vector of manipulated inputs (heater power, vent opening, screen position). |
| \(w(t)\) | **Process disturbance** (unmodelled heat gains, sensor drift, etc.). |
| \(v(t)\) | **Measurement noise** on the observed outputs. |
| \(y(t)\) | Measured outputs (temperature, humidity, CO₂). |
| \(C\) | Output matrix (selects the observable components). |
| \(\mathcal{K}(\cdot)\) | The known *controller* (digital, possibly PI/PID). |

The **continuous‑time** ODE model (discretised at the 5‑minute sampling period, \(\Delta=5\) min) is  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot x(t) &= f\bigl(x(t),u(t),\theta\bigr) + w(t) ,\\
y(t) &= C\,x(t) + v(t) .
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

Because the system runs in closed loop, the inputs are generated by the controller

\[
u(t) = \mathcal K\!\bigl(y(t),\; r(t)\bigr),
\tag{2}
\]

where \(r(t)\) are the **reference signals** (the set‑points for temperature, humidity, CO₂).  
Equation (2) makes the inputs *statistically dependent* on the disturbances \(w(t),v(t)\); consequently a naïve OE estimator that treats \(u(t)\) as exogenous will be **biased** (Forssell & Ljung, 1999).

---

### 2.2  Why the ordinary output‑error (OE) estimator fails  

The OE estimator solves  

\[
\hat\theta_{\text{OE}} = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{k=1}^{N}\| y(k) - C\hat x(k;\theta) \|^2 ,
\tag{3}
\]

where \(\hat x(k;\theta)\) is the simulated state trajectory obtained by integrating (1) **with the measured inputs** \(u(k)\) and *ignoring* any noise model.  

Because \(u(k)\) depends on \(y(k)\), the term \(C\hat x(k;\theta)\) is *correlated* with the very disturbances we are trying to “explain”. The minimisation therefore drives the parameters toward values that **absorb part of the disturbance** (i.e., a biased solution).  

The bias does not disappear even with infinitely long data, unless the disturbance never reaches the inputs (the special case where the controller is *feed‑forward* only).

---

### 2.3  General remedy: **Closed‑loop prediction‑error (PE) identification**  

The classic solution for linear systems is the **prediction‑error method (PEM)** with an explicit stochastic noise model and, if needed, instrumental variables.  
The same idea extends to nonlinear ODEs.  

#### 2.3.1  Augment the model with a stochastic noise description  

Add a *dynamic* noise model (usually a linear filter) that captures the correlation structure of \(w(t)\) and \(v(t)\). A minimal but effective choice is a **first‑order ARMA** (or a low‑order AR) process:

\[
\begin{aligned}
e(t) &\triangleq y(t) - C\,x(t) ,\\
e(t) &= H(q,\psi) \, e(t) + v(t), \qquad H(q,\psi) = \frac{B(q,\psi)}{A(q,\psi)},
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

where \(q^{-1}\) is the forward shift operator, \(\psi\) are the noise‑model parameters, and \(v(t)\) is assumed white Gaussian.  

The **full stochastic model** (sometimes called a *state‑space‑with‑noise* model) becomes  

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\dot x(t) &= f\bigl(x(t),u(t),\theta\bigr) + w(t),\\
e(t)      &= H(q,\psi)\, v(t),\\
y(t)      &= C\,x(t) + e(t),\\
u(t)      &= \mathcal K\!\bigl(y(t), r(t)\bigr) .
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5}
\]

#### 2.3.2  One‑step‑ahead predictor (the quantity that the PE cost uses)  

From (5) we can write the **one‑step‑ahead predictor** of the output, conditional on all past data \(\mathcal I_{t-1}\):

\[
\hat y(t|t-1;\theta,\psi) = C\,\hat x(t|t-1) + \hat e(t|t-1),
\tag{6}
\]

where  

* \(\hat x(t|t-1)\) is obtained by integrating the ODE **with the *predicted* input**  

  \[
  \hat u(t-1) = \mathcal K\!\bigl(\hat y(t-1|t-2), r(t-1)\bigr),
  \]

  i.e. we propagate the controller **inside** the predictor, not with the measured \(u\).  

* \(\hat e(t|t-1)\) is the output of the linear filter \(H(q,\psi)\) applied to past prediction errors (the usual Kalman‑type predictor for an ARMA filter).

The predictor is **unbiased** because the input used inside the predictor is *generated* by the *same* controller that generated the real inputs, but it depends only on **past** data – the future disturbance cannot influence it.

#### 2.3.3  Prediction‑error cost  

Define the **prediction error**

\[
\varepsilon(t;\theta,\psi) \;=\; y(t) - \hat y(t|t-1;\theta,\psi) .
\tag{7}
\]

The **PEM estimator** solves  

\[
(\hat\theta_{\text{PE}},\hat\psi_{\text{PE}}) \;=\;
\arg\min_{\theta,\psi} \; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\|\varepsilon(t;\theta,\psi)\|^{2}.
\tag{8}
\]

Because the predictor respects the closed‑loop relationship (2), the estimator is **consistent** under the usual regularity conditions (identifiability, persistent excitation, correct noise model order).
