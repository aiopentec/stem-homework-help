### 1. Restate What's Being Asked in Plain Language

When drawing resonance structures for a benzene ring that has a single substituent that donates electrons (a $+M$ or electron-donating group like $-\text{OH}$ or $-\text{Cl}$), the process is straightforward because that one group dictates the electron flow. 

However, when a benzene ring has **two different $+M$ groups** attached to it—such as in **chlorophenol** (which has both a hydroxyl group, $-\text{OH}$, and a chlorine atom, $-\text{Cl}$)—things get more complicated. The student wants to know:
1. **Which** of the two $+M$ groups actually pushes its lone pair into the ring to initiate resonance? 
2. **How** do you draw the combined resonance structures?
3. **Whose** electron donation makes the overall molecule more stable?

---

### 2. Step-by-Step Worked Solution

Let's use **4-chlorophenol** (where the $-\text{OH}$ and $-\text{Cl}$ groups are para to each other) as our primary example to understand how two $+M$ groups interact with a benzene ring.

#### Step 1: Identify the nature of both substituents
* **Hydroxyl group ($-\text{OH}$):** Oxygen has lone pairs and is strongly $+M$ (electron-donating via resonance) due to the strong overlap of its $2p$ orbital with the carbon $2p$ orbital of the benzene ring. It is also $-I$ (electron-withdrawing via induction), but its resonance effect dominates when considering electron donation.
* **Chlorine atom ($-\text{Cl}$):** Chlorine also has lone pairs and is $+M$ (electron-donating via resonance). However, because chlorine is a Period 3 element, its $3p$ orbital is larger and overlaps less efficiently with the carbon $2p$ orbital compared to oxygen. Furthermore, chlorine is much more electronegative than carbon, making its $-I$ (inductive) effect very strong, often outweighing its weak $+M$ effect in overall reactivity.

#### Step 2: Answer "Which group shares its lone pair?"
**Both groups can potentially share their lone pairs into the ring**, because both are $+M$ substituents. However, **they do not do so equally or simultaneously** to the same degree. 

* The $-\text{OH}$ group is a **much stronger $+M$ donor** than the $-\text{Cl}$ group. 
* Therefore, the resonance structures where the **oxygen atom** donates its lone pair to create a carbon-oxygen double bond are the primary contributors to the overall resonance hybrid. 
* The chlorine atom *also* acts as a $+M$ donor, but its contribution is much weaker. You must be able to draw resonance structures initiated by **both** groups to fully understand the system, but recognize that the $-\text{OH}$ driven structures dominate.

#### Step 3: Draw the resonance structures driven primarily by the stronger $+M$ group ($-\text{OH}$)
Let's assume an *ortho/para* relationship, specifically 4-chlorophenol. We will push electrons starting from the $-\text{OH}$ group:

1. **Structure 1 (Neutral):** The starting molecule. Oxygen has lone pairs; chlorine has lone pairs.
2. **Structure 2 (Ortho negative charge):** The lone pair on the $-\text{OH}$ oxygen moves down to form a double bond between oxygen and the ipso-carbon. The $\pi$ bond of the benzene ring between the ortho and meta carbons shifts onto the ortho carbon as a lone pair (negative charge). 
   * *Note:* Chlorine is sitting at the para position, unaffected in this specific canonical form, though it experiences an overall electron-rich system.
3. **Structure 3 (Para negative charge):** The negative charge on the ortho carbon moves to form a double bond with the adjacent ring carbon, forcing the next ring $\pi$ bond to shift onto the para carbon. 
   * *Crucial moment:* When the negative charge reaches the **para carbon** (the carbon bonded to $-\text{Cl}$), this carbon is now electron-rich adjacent to the chlorine atom.
4. **Structure 4 (Ortho negative charge):** The negative charge shifts around the ring to the other ortho position.
5. **Structure 5:** The electrons return to oxygen, restoring the neutral $-\text{OH}$ group and reforming the aromatic ring $\pi$ bonds.

*(You can independently draw a secondary set of minor resonance structures where the chlorine atom pushes its lone pair into the ring in the exact same sequence, resulting in minor canonical forms where $\text{Cl}$ bears a positive charge).*

#### Step 4: Determine whose sharing makes the molecule more stable
* **Kinetic/Electronic Dominance ($-\text{OH}$):** Oxygen is smaller and less electronegative relative to its ability to share $\pi$ electron density smoothly into the carbon framework. The resonance structures where **oxygen** donates electrons and places negative charges on the ring carbons are **more stable** (lower energy) than those where chlorine donates electrons, because oxygen accommodates a positive charge much better than chlorine (oxygen is more basic/less aggressively electronegative in terms of holding onto a positive formal charge than $Cl^+$).
* **Overall Stability:** The simultaneous presence of two $+M$ groups makes the benzene ring **strongly activated** toward electrophilic aromatic substitution, with the $-\text{OH}$ group acting as the dominant director because of its superior orbital overlap and stronger $+M$ power.

### 3. Final Answer

When two $+M$ groups (like $-\text{OH}$ and $-\text{Cl}$) are present on a benzene ring:
1. **Both groups share their lone pairs** into the ring via resonance, because both are $+M$ substituents.
2. **The stronger $+M$ group dictates the primary resonance behavior.** In chlorophenol, the $-\text{OH}$ group is a much stronger $+M$ donor than the $-\text{Cl}$ group due to better $2p-2p$ orbital overlap with the carbon ring.
3. **Stability:** The resonance structures initiated by the $-\text{OH}$ group are major contributors to the hybrid and provide greater stability to the intermediates because oxygen handles the resulting positive formal charge much better than the larger, more electronegative chlorine atom.

### 4. Common Mistakes

* **Mistake 1: Assuming only one group can do resonance.** Students often think the "stronger" group shuts down the other. In reality, *both* groups possess lone pairs and can independently initiate resonance structures; one is simply a major contributor and the other is a minor contributor.
* **Mistake 2: Forgetting orbital size (Period 2 vs. Period 3).** Students frequently treat $-\text{Cl}$ and $-\text{OH}$ as having equal $+M$ strengths because both are single-bonded to the ring with lone pairs. Always remember that Period 3 elements ($\text{Cl}$, $\text{Br}$) have poor orbital overlap with carbon $2p$ orbitals, making their $-I$ (inductive) effect much more dominant than their $+M$ effect.
* **Mistake 3: Pushing electrons *towards* the $+M$ group.** Remember that $+M$ groups are **electron donors**. Electrons must always flow *from* the lone pairs of the substituent *into* the $\pi$ system of the benzene ring, never the other way around during these resonance steps.
