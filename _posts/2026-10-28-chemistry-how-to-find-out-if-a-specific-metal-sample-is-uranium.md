### 2. Step-by-Step Solution

*Safety Warning: Uranium is a radioactive, heavy-metal toxin. Handling raw, unsealed uranium metal—especially attempting to dissolve, sand, or heat it—poses severe health risks via inhalation, ingestion, and radiological exposure. The following steps outline the theoretical and historical chemical identification of uranium for educational purposes.*

#### Step 1: Understand the Physical and Chemical Properties of Uranium
To reliably identify uranium without radiometric equipment (like a Geiger counter or scintillation detector), we must rely on its unique chemical reactivity. 
* **Appearance:** Freshly cut uranium metal is silvery-white, but it rapidly oxidizes in air to form a dark, adherent oxide layer. 
* **Reactivity:** Uranium is a moderately strong reducing agent. It reacts with acids, water (slowly at room temperature, rapidly when heated), and halogens.
* **Characteristic Chemistry:** Uranium forms distinctively colored complexes in solution, particularly yellow, green, and deep red-brown depending on its oxidation state.

#### Step 2: Perform a Controlled Dissolution Test (Chemical Isolation)
Unlike tungsten (which the user noted has a similar density of $19.25 \ g/cm^3$ and is notoriously difficult to dissolve in common household acids), uranium reacts readily with nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$).

1. **The Reaction:** When uranium metal is placed in concentrated or moderately strong nitric acid, it dissolves rapidly, producing brown nitrogen dioxide gas ($\text{NO}_2$) and forming soluble uranyl nitrate ($\text{UO}_2(\text{NO}_3)_2$).
   $$\text{U (s)} + 8\text{HNO}_3\text{ (aq)} \rightarrow \text{UO}_2(\text{NO}_3)_2\text{ (aq)} + 4\text{NO}_2\text{ (g)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{ (l)}$$
2. **Distinction from Tungsten:** Tungsten metal is largely inert to nitric acid due to the formation of a protective tungsten trioxide ($\text{WO}_3$) layer. If the metal completely resists nitric acid under conditions where uranium should react, it is likely tungsten or another noble metal, not uranium.

#### Step 3: Perform the Fluorescent Bead Test (Borax Bead Test)
Historically, uranium can be identified by the distinct color it imparts to a borax or sodium fluoride bead when heated in a flame.

1. **Preparation:** Create a small loop in a nichrome or platinum wire. Heat the loop and dip it into borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) until a clear, glassy bead forms in the loop.
2. **Introduction of Sample:** Touch the hot bead to a tiny amount of the dissolved or powdered metal salt (or an oxidized surface scraping).
3. **Flame Test:** Heat the bead in the oxidizing (outer) zone of a gas flame.
4. **Observation:** 
   * In the **oxidizing flame**, a uranium-infused borax bead turns **yellow to yellowish-green** while hot, and becomes a clear, bright **yellow-green** when cool.
   * *Note on Enrichment:* This specific chemical test **cannot** distinguish between depleted, natural, or enriched uranium. Chemical properties (such as color and reactivity) depend on the element's electron configuration, which is identical regardless of the isotope ($\text{U-238}$ vs. $\text{U-235}$).

#### Step 4: Address the Enriched vs. Depleted Question
The user asked if there is a way to test if the sample is enriched.
* **Chemical Test Limitations:** Chemical tests **cannot** determine isotopic enrichment. U-238 and U-235 undergo the exact same chemical reactions because they have the same number of protons and electrons.
* **Non-Radiometric Physical Test (Mass Spectrometry):** To determine enrichment without a Geiger counter, one would need access to a Gas Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer (IRMS). This requires turning the uranium into uranium hexafluoride ($\text{UF}_6$) gas—a highly dangerous industrial process—to measure the minute mass difference between U-235 and U-238 atoms. 
* **Conclusion for Home Testing:** It is **impossible** to test for uranium enrichment at home without specialized nuclear or analytical instrumentation (such as a High-Purity Germanium detector for gamma spectrometry or a mass spectrometer).

### 3. Final Answer

1. **To identify uranium metal without a Geiger counter:** You must use its distinct chemical reactivity. Uranium reacts vigorously with nitric acid to form a yellow-green solution of uranyl nitrate (unlike tungsten, which resists nitric acid). Furthermore, melting a small trace of the sample into a borax bead and heating it in an oxidizing flame produces a characteristic bright yellow-green, fluorescent bead.
2. **To test for enrichment:** It is **impossible** to test for enrichment at home. Chemical reactivity is identical for all uranium isotopes, meaning advanced analytical equipment (such as mass spectrometry or gamma-ray spectrometry) is strictly required to differentiate between depleted, natural, and enriched uranium.

### 4. Common Mistakes

* **Assuming Density is Definitive:** Relying solely on density calculations (e.g., measuring mass and volume) will fail to differentiate uranium from tungsten, as their densities are within $\approx 1\%$ of each other.
* **Attempting Chemical Enrichment Tests:** Assuming that because enriched uranium has different nuclear properties it will also behave differently in chemical reactions (like acid tests or color tests). Isotopes share identical chemical properties.
* **Neglecting Severe Safety Hazards:** Attempting to grind, sand, or dissolve uranium metal at home. Uranium dust is pyrophoric (can spontaneously catch fire in air) and highly toxic if inhaled or ingested, posing heavy metal poisoning and radiological risks.
