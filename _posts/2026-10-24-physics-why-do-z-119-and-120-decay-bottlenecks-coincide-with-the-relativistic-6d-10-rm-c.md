When scientists try to synthesize new, ultra-heavy elements (like Element 119 and Element 120), these atoms are unstable and decay step-by-step into lighter elements by emitting alpha particles (helium nuclei, which decrease the atomic number $Z$ by 2). Ideally, these decay chains continue safely via alpha decay until they reach a known, longer-lived isotope. However, these chains often get cut short by **spontaneous fission (SF)**—where the nucleus simply splits violently in half, halting the identification of the element.

### 2. Step-by-Step Derivation & Explanation

To understand why nuclear stability limits (decay bottlenecks) line up with electronic subshell closures, we must bridge **nuclear shell effects** (stability against fission) with **relativistic electronic structure** (quantum-electrodynamic boundaries).

#### Step 1: Understand the Role of Shell Closures in Nuclear Fission
Spontaneous fission half-lives depend exponentially on the height and width of the fission barrier, which is heavily dictated by **nuclear shell closures** (magic numbers). 
* Just as closed electronic shells give noble gases chemical stability, closed *nucleon* shells (protons and neutrons) give atomic nuclei spherical stability, raising their fission barriers.
* As we move away from closed nuclear shells, nuclei become softer, more deformed, and their fission barriers drop sharply, making spontaneous fission much more probable than alpha decay.

#### Step 2: The Relativistic Shift in Superheavy Elements ($Z \ge 100$)
As nuclear charge $Z$ increases, the inner-shell electrons approach relativistic speeds ($v \approx c$), causing two major perturbations:
1. **Direct Relativistic Contraction:** S- and $p_{1/2}$ orbitals contract radially and drop significantly in energy.
2. **Indirect Expansion/Spin-Orbit Splitting:** Higher-angular-momentum orbitals ($d$ and $f$) are destabilized and split widely due to spin-orbit coupling (e.g., $6d_{3/2}$ vs $6d_{5/2}$).

Because of these extreme relativistic effects, the filling order of electrons departs radically from standard Madelung rules. The boundaries where specific subshells (like $6d^{10}$ at $Z=112$ for Copernicium) finish filling represent points of maximum electron-density symmetry and stabilization in the relativistic Dirac-Fock framework.

#### Step 3: The Proton-Neutron / Electronic Structural Isomorphism
Why do the electronic subshell boundaries (like $Z=112$, $Z=104$) align with nuclear decay bottlenecks? 

1. **Coulomb Potential Scaling:** Both the electronic Dirac-Fock Hamiltonian and the macroscopic part of the nuclear liquid-drop/shell model depend fundamentally on the central Coulomb potential generated by the proton charge distribution $Z$. 
2. **Quantum Number Symmetries:** As Logvinovich and Perez demonstrate using $SO(3) \to O_h$ group-branching metrics, the capacities of relativistic subshells $d_l = 2(2l+1)$ dictate periodic "floors" or major structural closures. Because nuclear shell models use very similar spherical harmonic oscillator potentials with spin-orbit terms ($\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{s}$), the nodal structures of the underlying wavefunctions share an isomorphic mathematical skeleton.
3. **The Microscopic Origin of the Bottleneck:** When an alpha-decay chain descends from $Z=120$ or $119$, it crosses regions of varying single-particle level densities. The sudden onset of spontaneous fission at $Z=112$ (Copernicium) and $Z=104$ (Rutherfordium) corresponds precisely to the exhaustion or drastic reorganization of major deformed proton subshell gaps (specifically, gaps associated with quadrupole and hexadecrapole deformations near the deformed "island of stability"). These proton numbers mirror the completion of relativistic electronic blocks because the same underlying relativistic quantum mechanics govern both the electronic shell closures and the nucleonic single-particle potentials at extreme $Z$.

### 3. Final Answer

The coincidence between $Z=112$ / $Z=104$ nuclear decay bottlenecks and relativistic subshell boundaries ($6d^{10}$ and $6d/5f^{14}$) occurs because **both atomic electronic structures and nuclear shell potentials are governed by the same underlying relativistic quantum mechanics and central Coulomb field geometry.** 

Specifically:
1. **Electronic side:** $Z = 112$ marks the completion of the relativistic $6d^{10}$ subshell (Copernicium), a major structural boundary in the Dirac-Fock periodic table.
2. **Nuclear side:** The single-particle nucleon potentials feature spin-orbit splittings and shell structures mathematically isomorphic to the spherical-to-octahedral group mappings of relativistic electron orbitals. 
3. **The Connection:** The exhaustion of these structural subshells directly coincides with a local minimum in nuclear shell stabilization (fission barriers), causing spontaneous fission to abruptly overtake alpha emission and create the observed decay bottlenecks.

### 4. Common Mistakes

* **Assuming zero physical connection:** Students often think electronic configurations (chemistry) and nuclear stability (physics) are entirely decoupled. In superheavy elements, extreme relativistic effects couple nuclear charge directly to quantum shell boundaries.
* **Confusing Alpha Decay with Spontaneous Fission:** Remember that $\alpha$-decay is a tunneling process dependent on $Q$-values and barrier penetrability, whereas SF depends on collective mass-asymmetry deformations. A "bottleneck" happens when SF half-lives drop faster than $\alpha$-decay half-lives, *not* because $\alpha$-decay stops working.
* **Ignoring Relativistic Corrections:** Using non-relativistic Hartree-Fock or standard Aufbau filling rules for $Z > 100$ will place subshell closures at completely incorrect atomic numbers, destroying the correlation with the nuclear data.
